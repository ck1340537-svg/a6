The Jewel Frontier materials guide

Precious metals, crystals, dial materials and gemstones explained, so you can understand exactly what you are looking at in a fine watch.

Precious metals

Understanding gold and platinum

Pure gold is too soft for a watch case, so it is alloyed with other metals. The karat number tells you how much gold is present: 24 karat is pure, 18 karat is 75% gold and 14 karat is about 58.5%.

Yellow gold is alloyed with silver and copper. Rose gold uses more copper for its pink warmth. White gold includes paler metals and is often plated with rhodium for extra brightness.

Platinum, usually 95% pure in watchmaking, is heavier and rarer than gold, with a naturally white colour that never fades. It develops a soft patina over time rather than shiny scratches.

Crystals

What protects the dial

  • Sapphire crystal: synthetic sapphire, extremely scratch resistant and very clear. Often treated with an anti-reflective coating.
  • Mineral glass: toughened glass that is affordable and impact resistant but scratches more easily.
  • Acrylic (hesalite): a plastic crystal found on many vintage watches. It scratches easily but can be polished smooth again.

A simple test: a sapphire crystal usually feels cooler to the touch than acrylic, and fine scratches are rare.

Guilloché

An engraved pattern of fine, repeating lines on a dial, catching light in a shimmering way.

Enamel

Powdered glass fused onto a dial at high temperature, creating deep, glossy colour that does not fade.

Meteorite & stone

Thin slices of natural materials such as meteorite or onyx make every dial unique.

Gem settings

How stones are set

Claw (prong) settings hold each stone with small metal claws and let in the most light. Bezel settings surround a stone with a metal rim for maximum security. Pavé and snow settings cover a surface with many small stones, the latter using different sizes to cover every gap.

Whatever the setting, stones should sit level, feel secure and show even spacing. Loose stones may click or rattle slightly when gently tapped.

Hardness

The Mohs scale, simply

The Mohs scale ranks how easily a material can be scratched, from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). Harder materials scratch softer ones, which is why storing pieces separately matters.

  • 10: diamond
  • 9: sapphire and ruby (corundum), sapphire crystal
  • About 5.5: typical glass
  • 2.5–4.5: pearl and mother-of-pearl
  • About 2.5–3: pure gold
